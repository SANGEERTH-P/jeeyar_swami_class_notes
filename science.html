Science and Vedanta Advaita and science
Where do we draw the line between science and Vedanta? 5 November 2026

Science often dismisses Vedanta as having no evidence and speaking a language that makes no sense. This discussion turned the question around in two steps: first, the fundamental problems inside science itself; then, honestly, the problems inside our own tradition. The aim is not to find fault with either side but to see the facts clearly, so that the two can work together.

1. Problems in science

Darwinian theory: data is not evidence

Darwin’s theory is often presented as proven, but what we actually have is data, and the theory is an interpretation of that data, at best the best interpretation available. We have observed adaptation, animals adjusting to their surroundings, but no animal has been observed turning into another animal. The reply that such change takes millions of years cannot itself be tested. When two organisms differ slightly, it is assumed that the one with fewer features evolved into the one with more. That is an interpretation, made partly because the possibility that both simply existed side by side is not considered.

Treating related organisms as connected can be a useful working assumption, and it does help research. That use is not disputed. But data read through a theory is not evidence for the theory.

Inductive reasoning: science also has faith

Most of physics rests on induction. We observe the same pattern many times, and the more often we see it, the more we believe it will continue. When challenged, the reply is “I can test it”, but a test is only one more observation.

Take a simple example. When you drop an object, you are not making it fall; you are only letting go of it. Whether it falls is not in your hands. Why it falls, we do not really know: one can say it is gravity, another can say it is the curvature of space-time, and later someone may say something else. What we know is that it has fallen a thousand times, so we expect it to fall again. That expectation is a belief. Saying “under these conditions it will fall” does not help, because we did not create those conditions and we do not control them.

In Indian terms this is anumana (inference), as distinct from pratyaksha (direct perception). Science believes and has faith. It accepts that faith because it lets research proceed, yet refuses the same allowance to yoga, meditation or Vedanta, claiming to accept only perception.

The observer is always inside the experiment

Physics claims to describe the world independently of any observer, yet every one of its statements comes from observation. After observing, it quietly removes the observer from the equation and announces “this is its nature”. The formulas, the logic and the mathematics applied to those observations do not exist out in the world; they come from the same mind that observed.

Units are a clear example. No metre exists in nature. When we say the earth travels so many kilometres per hour, the hour is defined by our own cycle of day and night. In a universal context, who defines a second? The speed of light is a speed only relative to the second and the metre that we chose. Even a computer does not calculate in any meaningful sense: it follows instructions and stores what it is given, and the data on its disk means nothing until a person makes sense of it. The biggest contributor to every equation, the observer, is the one that is left out.

The illusion of dimensions

We speak of one, two and three dimensions, but a straight line cannot even be defined in a way that holds up. A line is said to be a series of infinite points, and a point has no measure and no unit: it is only a location. Such a point can be imagined but never realised; anything we actually draw as a point is a three-dimensional object. So 1D and 2D exist only in the mind.

What we call 3D is the only dimension we ever meet, the physical one, which maps to the annamaya level. Our brain is designed to see the world in this format, and that design is what lets us conceive the rest.

Because every measurement is built on points that cannot be realised, there is a gap between what the mind conceives and what exists, and this gap shows up as irrational numbers. Area and distance are only counts of a unit we chose, such as how many millimetres fit between two points. But how can we measure something drawn with something that can never be drawn? Every line we draw is supposed to be made of points, yet a point has no size and cannot be drawn at all; so every length we measure is only an approximation of something that does not exist on paper. And for subtle things like energy and charge, dimensions make no sense at all.

Truth and untruth, real and unreal: outside physics

Above mathematics comes the question of what is true and what is not, what is real and what is unreal. Taking something unreal as real comes from a defect. We see a mirage and take it for water because we assume and do not test, and we do not test because we want it to be water. Here the mind enters, with its wishes, hopes and intentions, none of which appear in mathematics or in any physical theory.

Mistaking something is also different from telling a lie on purpose, and that difference belongs to psychology. Beyond this lie feeling and experience themselves, such as the taste of something, and the sense of “I”: the wish to go on living and to experience more and more happiness. Neuroscience studies the brain, but it does not reach what lies beyond the brain.

So the takeaway is this: truth and error, intention, experience and the sense of “I” are present in every observer who does physics, yet physics itself has no way to describe any of them.

Physics borrows from other domains

Physics has a clear boundary, and that boundary is mathematics. Physics uses numbers and groups but cannot explain them. A group or class, such as “human”, does not exist anywhere physically: we meet individual people, never “human” as such. Number and class are abstract, and physics has no way to venture into the abstract, however much interdisciplinary work speaks of the mathematics of physics and the physics of mathematics.

A human being moves through all these layers at once. We perceive, calculate, abstract, wish, intend, tell the truth, tell lies and experience. Science, by contrast, is divided into domains that cannot cross their own boundaries, and each has problems of its own. The layers make sense only together, and the thread that holds them together is the observer, the mind. Pull that thread and everything falls apart.

The point of listing these problems is not to find fault. It is to see the fact as it is.

2. Problems in Vedanta

Science mocks Vedanta: somebody knows some Sanskrit text, so why should we believe it? Our own tradition has three real problems, and they have to be admitted.

Real practitioners

The starting point of Vedanta is experience, and an experience, such as a taste or a colour, cannot be handed to another person. Its very nature is subjective. Our system also lacks enough genuine practitioners who have actually realised what the texts describe.

Science is subjective too. It is objective only among human beings: we do not know whether any other being understands gravity, or that one plus one is two, the way we do. Biology says humans understand alike because we share a common origin, but then why do we not all behave alike, and where does free will come in? Some things are common to all humans and some are not. And humanity is a very small sample.

Interpretation, even in the same language

Whatever means we use to describe an experience, the description can only be approximate. Even when the listener knows the same language, what they receive is their own interpretation, and because they have not had the experience themselves, the explanation carries a vagueness that cannot be removed. Unfortunately, even among people who know the language well, the interpretation is often poor.

Translation into other languages

When these ideas are translated into another language, the problem multiplies. The tanmatras are rendered smoothly as hearing, touch, sight, taste and smell, but such translations lose almost everything. Still, this is the best that can be done to express something so difficult.

3. Reading the tattvas afresh

A few examples show how the tattvas can be understood more precisely, and why they deserve respect.

Tejas and ap: what fuels the sun

Tejas is described as being of four kinds: bhauma, divya, audarya and akaraja. Leaving akaraja aside for now, the others are explained through what sustains them. Earthly fire is fed by parthiva fuel, while divya tejas, the light and heat of the sun, is fed by ap. This ap is not the water we drink; it is better understood as hydrogen. Today we know the sun is powered by the fusion of hydrogen. How would people who lived with firewood, without any modern instruments, have known this?

Shabda tanmatra: the wave nature

Shabda is usually taken to mean acoustic sound, the mechanical wave that needs air to travel. Critics then mock the idea that sound travels through akasha, since sound cannot travel in a vacuum while light can. The mistake is to assume that akasha is vacuum and that shabda is mechanical sound. Shabda tanmatra is the wave nature itself, and the whole manifest world rests on it. That is why everything has a wave nature, as science itself found through de Broglie’s work.

Sparsha and vayu: force

Vayu is not the wind; it is force. Force cannot be seen, only felt: if someone pushes you, you feel it because your body resists. That is why vayu has sparsha, touch. Shabda by itself does not move. It is a stationary wave, like a string tied at both ends and plucked. It moves only because, through panchikarana, sparsha is also present: when force is applied, the wave moves.

Rupa and tejas: the particle nature

Shabda tanmatra leads to sparsha tanmatra, which leads to rupa tanmatra and on to tejas, light and heat. So light has the wave nature, the force that moves it, and a particle nature that lets it be seen. Particles, dravya, are what we see; that is the rupa aspect.

Rasa and ap: the ability to react

Grosser still, matter can be tasted. Taste here does not mean flavour on the tongue; it means the ability to react. Light does not react, force does not react and a stationary wave does not react, but water does. When you taste something, it reacts with your saliva. Anything that reacts and dissolves comes under the nature of ap.

These mappings do not exist yet

Prithvi and gandha remain to be worked out, and all of these readings need much more enquiry. No such careful mappings exist today. Building them is how respect for Vedanta and its tattvas can be earned.

4. The computer and the question “who am I?”

Without even entering into consciousness, a simple question about memory shows the gap between a human being and a computer. A computer cannot forget: unless its physical memory is damaged, what it stores stays. Human design is complex beyond imagination and works at lightning speed, yet we can forget. Why? And why can we not memorise something at first sight? Reading it a hundred times is often not enough; we have to say it or repeat it. If we are so much more than a computer, why can we not simply store and replay whatever we hear?

On the other hand, the more we think, the more answers we find. A person can ask: who am I, actually? Am I this mind, this intellect? We can ask because we cut across all the layers: physical reality, abstraction, psychology with its wishes and intentions, and experience. A computer cannot enquire into itself. One could write a loop that prints “who am I?” again and again, or prints “contemplating…” and pauses, but that is not thinking; it is processing instructions.

When this was put to GPT in class, its answers were telling. It said it can demonstrate reasoning but cannot establish whether it actually experiences the process of thinking. It can investigate what it is, but cannot establish that it directly apprehends itself. It can understand what it means to live, but cannot honestly claim to want to live, and it cannot independently will or want anything.

A person can say “I don’t need anything; leave me alone” and withdraw, while remaining alive. A computer that shuts down is simply off. It always waits for instructions. That which can enquire, want and choose is what we mean by “I”.

5. The way forward

Two things follow. On one side, Vedanta has to earn respect through clear explanation of its tattvas. On the other, science has to be shown that it has serious problems within its own domain, and asked to stop commenting carelessly on ours. Physics carries no guarantee and is full of interpretation, yet it works. No one denies that it does wonders and benefits society. But Vedanta is the deeper of the two, and in it realisation is possible through knowledge itself.

Our own side needs cleaning up too. There are many false teachers who call themselves practitioners and claim realisation. We have to define the responsibility of our own group, identify who is truly learned and serious, and keep the enquiry genuine. It is also time to give Advaita a break, because when everything other than the self is treated as unreal, the large part of the Vedas that deals with the tattvas is brushed aside. The part of Vedanta to explore is the part that enquires into the observer. Karma can wait; for now the focus can be on questions that can be tested, such as sukha and dukha, how we behave, and how food affects behaviour.
