Questions on Advaita

November 2026

Advaita holds that Brahman alone is real and that the many things we see are appearances brought about by Maya. This discussion took that position seriously and asked it a series of simple questions: where Maya sits, who sees the appearances, and how anything can relate to anything else if there is only one. Each question exposes a gap, and together they point to a different answer: a plurality that is real and related.

1. Where does Maya reside?

If Maya is in the jiva, it is not in Brahman, and then there is already a second entity, something other than Brahman. If Maya is placed on Brahman itself, how can it spoil or veil Brahman? Brahman has no dosha, no defect, for anything to take hold of. And since Advaita says Brahman has no qualities but is itself the quality, placing Maya on Brahman ends with “Brahman is Maya”. But Brahman is not Maya. So the question stands: what is the locus of Maya? Where does it sit?

Maya has no locus standi at all. If it is outside Brahman, it is a separate reality. If it sits on Brahman, either Maya wins or Brahman wins. Should we then say Maya is Brahman, or that Maya has Brahman, or that Maya influences Brahman? If Maya influences Brahman, which part of Brahman is affected: the observer part? What did Brahman once observe properly that it now cannot observe because of Maya?

2. A Brahman that cannot know

If Brahman is the only one, why would it want to know anything? There is nothing for it to know and no one to know about; knowing itself makes no sense. Advaita also says Brahman does not experience ananda but is ananda: satyam is Brahman, jnanam is Brahman, ananda is Brahman. So in the paramarthika state Brahman has no experience at all. There is nothing other than Brahman, and it does not experience, think or feel. That is almost the description of achit, insentient matter.

3. The last observer and shunya

Such a view removes even the last and only observer. Anything that exists but cannot be observed by anyone is, for all purposes, shunya, a void. When the observer in you disappears, where do you go? Into shunya. That is nirvana: you are simply not there to experience anything.

There is a parallel in quantum physics, which says that in the absence of any observer the universe exists as a continuous, uniform wave function, with nothing distinct in it. Either way the result is the same. If you are the only one, you are in effect zero. Having only one is as good as having none.

4. If anything exists outside me

Advaita’s own reason for insisting on one reality is a genuine problem. Suppose something exists outside me. How do I interact with it? Unless we share some common understanding of what interaction is, we cannot interact, and then it is as good as not existing for me. Worse, if something can exist outside me, I am bounded. What binds me? What puts the boundary around me, and what lies beyond it, if I am not ananta, infinite? Can I even comprehend that outside, whether we call it void or anything else?

So the reasoning runs: if there are two realities, they cannot talk to each other, so there must be only one. This is a real difficulty, and teachers of Advaita state it clearly. But as the earlier sections show, a single reality with nothing related to it cannot be observed, cannot know and cannot even count as one.

5. What is awareness?

To escape this, one may say the one reality has awareness. Then the next question is: aware of what, and of whom? If it is awareness of itself, what does that mean? Knowing is a process, a mechanism. So we must ask in what form it knows itself, where this ability to know resides, and where the process is carried out. Maya answers none of these questions.

6. Appearance to whom?

The usual reply is that the knower, the instrument of knowledge and the knowledge are all appearances of one underlying reality. But appearances must reside somewhere, and they must appear to someone. If they appear to Brahman, Brahman is seeing itself this way. If all differences (bheda) are avidya, then Brahman is the ashraya, the host, of that avidya, which is a serious problem.

And what was observed before? In the paramarthika state there is no “I” at all, by Advaita’s own answer. Then suddenly there are many plural objects because Maya has interfered. Where did Maya come from? Where, and how, does it meet Brahman? There is no clarity on this.

7. Why the dream example fails

The world is often compared to a dream, or to a movie on a screen: it appears, but it is not true, since truth is defined as trikala abadhya, what is not contradicted at any of the three times. But a dream needs an observer first, and it is made from what that observer has already experienced.

People born blind do not dream in pictures. Their dreams contain sound, touch, smell, taste, emotion and a sense of space, but never sight, because they have never seen. A dream cannot contain what was never experienced. So if Brahman alone existed, with no experience of plurality, from what would it dream a plural world? If there are many real things, relating them is no problem. If there is only one, plurality cannot even be conceived.

8. The relationship between Maya and Brahman

If Maya interacts with Brahman, the two are related, and through that relation they know each other. Then the relationship has to be defined. The moment it is defined, Brahman is no longer alone, because it is related to something. Duality is already there. However it is phrased, “it is only because of Maya that there is plurality” brings in a second entity, and a relationship that must be explained.

9. The answer: a related plurality

The way out of the puzzle is neither a single reality nor many unrelated ones, but a plurality that is fully related and connected. This is Visishtadvaita: distinct, but not dual. Things are distinct from one another, no doubt, yet they are related, and form one connected whole.

The common factor that makes relation possible is the antaryami, the Lord who dwells within everything. Think of electricity running a fan and a bulb. I did not make the fan or the bulb, yet I can switch them on, because the same current connects us all. We are a unified reality, not “one reality”. Something wholly unrelated to everything else cannot exist.

One is the known, another is the knower, and a third is needed for any communication between them. The relation, the interaction itself, becomes a third entity, because something passes from this to that. So counting what is real starts with three, not with one.

10. Interaction needs a protocol

Consider two people talking. If we communicate through sound, I must be able to produce sound and you must be able to receive it. But it is not only sending and hearing: it is knowing and understanding that particular sound, which is much more than saying “I understood”. It is not just an interaction or a relationship; it is a complete protocol.

Then come further questions. If we are related through sound, how am I related to sound? Why should I be able to hear at all? There is the sense that detects sound, and then there is understanding or experiencing it, and my understanding may differ from yours. To reach a common understanding we would say, “let us talk”, but talking is the very interaction we are trying to explain. There is much here to think about.

Realisation through knowledge

This is why the enquiry begins with knowledge. Realisation through direct vision, sakshatkara, belongs to yoga and is a higher stage. But realisation through knowledge comes first, and it is open to anyone willing to think these questions through.
